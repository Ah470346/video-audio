# Dialogue, Subtext, Narrator-Led Compression, and Xung Ho

Use this reference when the manuscript contains direct dialogue, unclear speakers, unnatural Vietnamese speech, inconsistent forms of address, or scenes that may need narration instead of quoted lines.

## First Question: Should This Be Direct Dialogue?

Direct dialogue is valuable when it changes story state. It should usually do at least one of these:

- force a decision;
- change a relationship;
- reveal a lie, clue, threat, confession, refusal, promise, or betrayal;
- show character under pressure;
- create subtext between what is said and what is meant;
- alter power, knowledge, intimacy, or danger.

Summarize dialogue when it only:

- handles greetings and logistics;
- repeats what narration already said;
- delivers backstory both characters already know;
- fills time between real beats;
- explains feelings that could be shown through action;
- exists only so the author can avoid narration.

Narrator-led audio often improves when routine exchanges are compressed. Do not demand theatrical dialogue in every scene.

## Dialogue Value Test

For each exchange, ask:

1. What does each speaker want from the other person right now?
2. What are they hiding?
3. What tactic are they using: evade, probe, flatter, threaten, bargain, joke, deflect, accuse, surrender?
4. What new information, choice, or relationship state exists after the exchange?
5. If the exchange is paraphrased, what is lost?

If nothing is lost, recommend compression into narration.

Example:

Weak direct dialogue:

> "Chào anh, hôm nay anh đến đây có việc gì?"
> "Tôi đến để hỏi về bản hợp đồng mà tuần trước chúng ta đã nhắc đến."

Compression:

> Anh bỏ qua lời chào, đặt thẳng bản hợp đồng lên bàn.

Why: the compression preserves function and starts closer to pressure.

## Natural Vietnamese Dialogue

Check:

- each line responds to what was actually said;
- characters do not recite facts both already know;
- speaker vocabulary matches age, rank, education, region, intimacy, and current emotion;
- lines can be spoken in one breath unless interruption is intentional;
- contractions, fragments, silence, and evasion are allowed when appropriate;
- characters do not all speak in the narrator's polished cadence;
- exposition is hidden inside tactic, conflict, denial, or accusation.

Sượng example:

> "Anh trai à, kể từ khi bố mất năm năm trước và để lại công ty cho anh, em luôn ghen tị với anh."

Stronger direction:

> "Năm năm rồi mà chiếc ghế của bố vẫn chỉ vừa với một mình anh nhỉ?"

Why: the second line carries history, resentment, relationship, and subtext without announcing all facts.

## Subtext

Subtext means the spoken line is a tactic, not a report.

Useful patterns:

- ask a practical question to test loyalty;
- joke to hide fear;
- use politeness to humiliate;
- call someone by title instead of intimate address to create distance;
- answer a different question to avoid confession;
- mention an object instead of the wound it represents;
- stay silent at the moment a sincere answer is expected.

Bad subtext is merely vague. Good subtext gives enough context for the listener to infer pressure.

Example:

Direct:

> "Em vẫn còn giận anh vì anh đã bỏ em ở bệnh viện."

Subtext:

> "Bệnh viện đổi màu sơn rồi. Chắc anh cũng không nhận ra đâu."

Use subtext only when the listener has enough setup to understand the buried meaning.

## Speaker Clarity for Audio

Audio cannot rely on paragraph breaks alone. Check:

- after three or more turns, can listeners still know who is speaking?
- do action beats identify the speaker only when needed?
- are tags overused when two speakers alternate clearly?
- do names or kinship terms re-anchor long exchanges?
- are multiple same-gender characters in the same scene causing pronoun confusion?

Good action beats change meaning:

> "Anh tin em?" Mai hỏi.
> Hùng đặt chìa khóa xuống bàn. "Không."

Weak decorative beat:

> "Anh tin em?" Mai chớp mắt hỏi.
> Hùng hít một hơi thật sâu, ánh mắt phức tạp. "Không."

The second may work once, but repeated generic body beats become noise.

## Forms of Address

Build a relationship matrix:

| Direction/context | Self-reference | Addressee term | Third-person reference | Narrator term | Social effect | Triggered changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Create directional rows. `A -> B` and `B -> A` are not interchangeable, and the same person may be named differently by the narrator, by a lover, and by an employee speaking in public.

Review:

- age and generation;
- kinship and in-law relationships;
- rank, class, profession, and workplace hierarchy;
- public versus private setting;
- intimacy, conflict, estrangement, performance, and strategy;
- narrator reference versus direct address;
- regional or family-specific habits.
- who else can hear the exchange;
- whether the term is sincere, strategic, ironic, concealing, humiliating, or conciliatory;

Vietnamese forms of address are not neutral labels. They carry emotional and social force.

Treat person reference as a choice among personal pronouns, kinship terms, names, titles/status terms, and omission. The correct question is not only `Cặp xưng hô nào đúng?` but also `Nhân vật đang dựng quan hệ gì trước ai, trong hoàn cảnh nào?`

Common defects:

- `anh-em` changes to `tôi-cô` without a rupture or strategy;
- parent and child shift from `mẹ-con` to `tôi-bà` without an emotional trigger;
- character calls an elder by bare given name in a context where that implies contempt but the story treats it as neutral;
- narrator uses `nó` for a human character without intended contempt or familiar hierarchy;
- an employee uses intimate address in public before relationship permits it;
- a character refers to a spouse with a cold title in narration while the POV claims tenderness.

Address shifts can be powerful when triggered:

- betrayal: `anh-em` -> `tôi-cô`;
- humiliation: title becomes bare name;
- reconciliation: cold title returns to kinship term;
- public performance: lovers use formal names to hide intimacy;
- power play: a superior uses `cô` instead of role/title to reduce status.

Also test asymmetry. One person may use `em-anh` while the other uses `tôi-cô`; this can deliberately encode pursuit, rejection, hierarchy, or refusal to recognize intimacy. Do not force reciprocity unless the story frames the asymmetry as accidental.

Problem:

> Earlier: "Em cứ tin anh."
> Later in a neutral scene: "Cô đưa hồ sơ cho tôi."

Diagnosis: emotional distance changes without a precipitating event.

Repair direction: restore the prior address, establish a rupture, or make the cold address a deliberate tactic visible in the scene.

## Pronouns and Referents

Audio listeners need obvious referents. Flag:

- long runs of `nó`, `hắn`, `anh ấy`, `cô ấy`, `người đó`;
- two men or two women in one paragraph with no name re-anchor;
- pronoun shifts that imply a changed emotional stance by accident;
- narrator using the wrong relationship noun;
- a quoted line where `anh`, `em`, `chị`, `cô`, `chú`, `bác` could point to multiple people.

Repair options:

- repeat the name;
- repeat the relationship noun: `người mẹ chồng`, `cậu em trai`, `ông giám đốc`;
- move the subject earlier;
- split the sentence;
- add a short action beat that identifies the speaker.

## Dialogue Diagnosis Labels

Use precise labels:

- **Expository dialogue:** characters tell each other facts both know.
- **Interchangeable voice:** any character could say the line.
- **No tactic:** speaker reports emotion but does not try to affect the other person.
- **False conflict:** argument repeats positions without changing power, knowledge, or decision.
- **Unclear speaker:** audio listener cannot track who says the line.
- **Address drift:** form of address changes without trigger.
- **Over-tagging:** tags explain every emotion and slow the exchange.
- **Under-tagging:** speaker identity becomes ambiguous.
- **Narration-worthy:** direct quotation adds no dramatic value.

## Output Guidance

When reporting dialogue issues, separate:

- lines worth keeping;
- lines to compress into narration;
- lines requiring rewrite;
- unclear speaker turns;
- interchangeable character voices;
- xung ho rules that should govern the next draft.

Keep illustrative rewrites short. Do not rewrite an entire scene unless the user asks.

# Story Contract, Narrator, POV, and Scene Coherence

Use this reference when the review needs to establish the story contract, diagnose narrator-led structure, check POV, build a story bible, or decide whether scenes cohere.

## Story Contract

Before judging details, identify:

- genre and subgenre;
- protagonist and focal character;
- immediate goal and deeper desire;
- obstacle and antagonist force;
- stakes;
- main dramatic question;
- narrator type;
- point of view and tense;
- tone and target experience;
- whether the text is a complete story, episode, or chapter.

Write in Vietnamese:

> Đây là một truyện [thể loại] dành cho [đối tượng], theo chân [nhân vật] cố gắng [mục tiêu] trước [trở lực], với trải nghiệm chính là [lời hứa cảm xúc/thể loại].

Use the contract to avoid false criticism. A healing slice-of-life story does not need the same climax as revenge drama. A mystery must satisfy clue logic; a family melodrama must satisfy relational reckoning.

## Narrator-Led Audio

Narrator-led audio uses narration as the main engine. It is not inherently weaker than dialogue-heavy drama.

Good narrator-led prose:

- orients the listener quickly;
- compresses routine actions;
- dramatizes only decisive moments;
- repeats key names or objects strategically;
- gives inner meaning when needed;
- uses rhythm and pauses as part of emotional design;
- keeps the listener aware of who knows what.

Weak narrator-led prose:

- summarizes the actual turning point;
- reports emotional labels without behavior;
- hides who acts and why;
- lists events without causal links;
- gives backstory before listeners know why it matters;
- overexplains after each scene.

## POV Types

### First Person

Check:

- the narrator only knows what they could perceive, infer, remember, or learn later;
- retrospective knowledge is handled consistently;
- the voice remains personal rather than omniscient;
- self-deception, bias, and memory limits are clear when relevant.

Problem:

> Tôi không biết lúc đó anh đang nghĩ rằng chỉ cần tôi bước thêm một bước, anh sẽ giữ tôi lại.

This is a knowledge leak unless the narrator later learned it or frames it as speculation.

### Third-Person Limited

Check:

- scene perception stays with one focal character unless there is a marked break;
- thoughts belong to the focal character;
- other characters are inferred through behavior, tone, and action;
- vocabulary matches the focal lens.

Problem:

> Hùng mỉm cười, tin rằng Mai đã tha thứ. Mai nhìn anh và nghĩ anh vẫn ích kỷ như trước.

Repair direction: stay in Hùng's viewpoint and infer Mai through behavior, or start a clearly marked Mai-viewpoint scene.

### Omniscient Narrator

Omniscience is valid when controlled. Check:

- narrator has a stable attitude and voice;
- transitions between minds are deliberate;
- information reveal is paced for suspense;
- omniscience does not destroy mystery or emotional uncertainty;
- the narrator does not accidentally withhold obvious context merely to trick listeners.

### Observer Narrator

Check:

- inner states are inferred through external evidence;
- the camera-like limitation is consistent;
- the narration does not suddenly name private thoughts without a POV change.

## Scene Coherence

Segment the manuscript into scenes. For each scene, record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Entry condition | What state exists at the start? |
| Goal/need | What does someone want now? |
| Obstacle | What blocks or pressures that want? |
| Key event | What action, revelation, or choice occurs? |
| Turn/result | What changes by the end? |
| Emotion | What changes inside a key character? |
| Link | Why does the next scene follow? |
| Function | Why does the story need this scene? |

Flag scenes that leave story state unchanged, repeat a prior function, or exist only to explain information before listeners need it.

## Situation-Model and Boundary-Stack Test

At every scene or major event transition, check five dimensions that listeners use to maintain a coherent event model:

1. **Protagonist:** who is currently acting or being followed?
2. **Time:** when is this relative to the previous event?
3. **Space:** where are the relevant people and objects?
4. **Causality:** why does this event follow or interrupt the prior event?
5. **Intention:** what active goal, plan, or concern organizes the event?

A change in one dimension may need only a light cue. Changes in three or more dimensions within one or two sentences create a `boundary stack`; inspect whether the listener receives a stable anchor before new information arrives.

Do not require mechanical restatement. A sentence can orient several dimensions naturally:

> Sáng hôm sau, Lan trở lại bệnh viện một mình để tìm bản xét nghiệm Hùng đã giấu.

This anchors time, protagonist, space, goal, and a causal connection. Consult `research-basis.md` for the event-indexing evidence behind this test.

## Scene Entry and Exit

Scene openings should orient by ear:

- who is present;
- where they are;
- when this is relative to the prior scene;
- what pressure is active.

Scene endings should change something:

- decision;
- information;
- relationship;
- danger;
- emotional state;
- plan;
- power balance.

Weak exit:

> Cô không biết mọi chuyện rồi sẽ ra sao.

Stronger exit:

> Sáng mai cô vẫn phải ký vào tờ đơn ấy. Chỉ khác là bây giờ, cô biết người đặt bút trước mình là ai.

## Running Story Bible Method

Keep short internal notes:

### Characters

- name;
- aliases;
- relationship nouns;
- public identity;
- private desire;
- fear or wound;
- secret;
- current knowledge;
- current strategy.

### Relationships

- kinship, rank, intimacy;
- conflict source;
- normal address;
- broken address;
- public/private differences;
- latest change.

### Timeline

- major events in order;
- elapsed time;
- flashback markers;
- deadlines;
- travel and physical feasibility.

### Promises

- title promise;
- opening promise;
- mystery questions;
- revenge targets;
- romance obstacle;
- horror rule;
- clue;
- emotional debt.

### Goal States

For each major goal, record:

- owner;
- goal and stakes;
- current state: active / blocked / achieved / abandoned / replaced / forgotten;
- latest attempt and outcome;
- trigger for any state change;
- whether the listener received closure or a deliberate open loop.

Goal changes are not defects. Untriggered replacement and author-forgotten goals are.

Use these notes to avoid judging only by impression.

## Narrator Voice

Check whether the narrator voice is stable:

- intimate, sharp, comic, bitter, lyrical, plain, formal, folk-like, investigative, etc.;
- word choice fits education, age, region, genre, and distance from events;
- narration does not suddenly become generic promotional copy;
- narrator attitude toward characters remains legible;
- metaphors come from the story world or character lens when possible.

A deliberate voice shift can work when caused by time, trauma, revelation, or changed narrator position. Accidental voice drift should be flagged.

## Exposition

Exposition is not bad. Bad exposition arrives before the listener has a reason to care or replaces a scene that should change the story.

Good exposition:

- answers a question the scene has raised;
- creates pressure;
- changes interpretation of a current action;
- is attached to a choice or conflict;
- is short enough for audio memory.

Weak exposition:

- opens with family/company/lore encyclopedia;
- names many people before any urgent relationship is established;
- pauses action to explain what both characters know;
- repeats information already shown;
- arrives during high urgency and kills momentum.

Repair options:

- delay until a character needs the information;
- distribute across conflict beats;
- convert to an object, habit, rumor, or changed address;
- compress into one vivid narrator sentence;
- place after a disturbance so listeners know why it matters.

## Reporting Narrator Issues

Use labels:

- POV leak;
- accidental mind-hop;
- narrator voice drift;
- exposition before relevance;
- summarized turning point;
- missing scene turn;
- weak scene bridge;
- scene without function;
- unclear focal character.

Always explain how the issue affects listening: confusion, reduced suspense, weak emotion, broken mystery fairness, or lost attachment.

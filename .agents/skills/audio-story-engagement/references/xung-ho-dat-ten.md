# Vietnamese Address Forms And Naming For Audio Stories

In Vietnamese, `xưng hô` does more than identify "who speaks to whom." It encodes relative age, rank, intimacy, power, attitude, and current emotion. A pair can shift from *anh-em* to *tôi-cô* without explanation; the shift itself is a relationship event.

> **Ear clarity first, relationship fit second, variety last.** Do not rotate names/pronouns merely to avoid repetition if listeners may be confused.

## 1. Three Layers Often Confused

### 1.1 Self-Reference

How the speaker calls themself: *con, cháu, em, anh, chị, cô, chú, tôi, tao, mình...*

### 1.2 Direct Address

How the speaker addresses the person they are speaking to: *mẹ, anh, chị, cô, ông, bà, cậu, mày, bạn...*

### 1.3 Third-Person Reference

How narrator/character refers to someone absent or not directly addressed: *mẹ tôi, chồng cô ấy, anh Minh, cô y tá, hắn, người đàn ông ấy...*

All three can differ in one scene:

> *“**Con** đã nói với **mẹ** rồi. **Anh Minh** không lấy số tiền đó.”*

`con-mẹ` is the dialogue pair; `Anh Minh` is third person. Agents often fail by spreading a pronoun list without identifying the layer.

## 2. Map Relationships By Pair

Before writing long dialogue, make a pair map, not only character profiles.

| Pair | A self/address B | B self/address A | Public | When cracked | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lan ↔ mother | con / mẹ | mẹ / con | same | tôi / bà only if rupture is extreme | mother favors younger sister |
| Lan ↔ husband | em / anh | anh / em | vợ tôi / chồng tôi | tôi / anh, tôi / cô | shift at betrayal |
| Lan ↔ younger sister | chị / em | em / chị | em gái tôi / chị tôi | tôi / cô or tao / mày | depends on class/voice |
| Lan ↔ lawyer | tôi / anh(chị) | tôi / chị | cô Lan / luật sư Duy | usually no shift | professional relation |

### Two-Way Test

A pair must make sense from both sides.

**Wrong:** younger sister uses *chị-em*, but older sister replies *tôi-cô* from the start without established distance.

**Intentional:** if the older sister has disowned her, the asymmetry should sound like conflict:

> *“Chị nghe em nói đã.”*
>
> *“Tôi không còn là chị của cô từ ngày cô ký tờ giấy đó.”*

Symmetry is not required; reason is.

## 3. Axes That Decide Address Forms

Before important scenes, check:

1. **Relative age** as the characters perceive it.
2. **Kinship/generation:** family role may override actual age.
3. **Status/power:** boss-employee, owner-guest, doctor-patient.
4. **Intimacy:** strangers, acquaintances, lovers, spouses, estranged people.
5. **Space:** in family/public vs private.
6. **Emotion/intent:** flattering, threatening, pleading, mocking, cutting off, reconciling.

One person can be referred to in many valid ways:

- *chồng tôi* to orient the listener;
- *anh* in intimate dialogue;
- *Vĩnh* when calling by name or opening distance;
- *anh ta* when speaking to outsiders with detachment;
- *người đàn ông ấy* when estrangement is intentional.

Do not hard-bind "one character = one pronoun."

## 4. Relationship Groups

These tables are starting points, not universal laws. Speech changes by region, generation, class, and story world.

### 4.1 Family

| Relationship | Common pair | Third-person reference |
|---|---|---|
| child ↔ parent | con-bố/mẹ; con-ba/má | bố tôi, mẹ tôi, bà ấy when distant intentionally |
| older sibling ↔ younger sibling | anh/chị-em | anh trai tôi, em gái tôi, given name when needed |
| grandchild ↔ elder relative | cháu-ông/bà/cô/chú/bác | bà ngoại tôi, chú út, cô Mai |
| wife ↔ husband | anh-em; mình-mình; tôi-anh/chị by couple | chồng tôi, vợ tôi, given name |

Names in family are allowed when natural:

- direct attention: *“Lan, nhìn mẹ.”*
- multiple siblings;
- parents calling children;
- relationship temperature shift;
- legal/evidence detail.

**Stiff:** *“Em gái tôi, hãy nghe chị nói.”*

**Natural:** *“Hạ, nghe chị một lần thôi.”*

In first-person narration, *em gái tôi* is useful for orientation; in direct dialogue, name or *em* is usually more natural.

### 4.2 Strangers And Social Relations

Do not force every stranger to have a name. Choose a label from what the narrator knows:

| Situation | Suggested reference |
|---|---|
| no name, brief encounter | người giao hàng, cô lễ tân, người đàn ông áo xám |
| profession/role matters | bác sĩ Phương, luật sư Duy, trưởng phòng Hà |
| equal, polite | tôi-anh/chị/bạn; name after introduction |
| clear age gap | cháu-cô/chú/bác; em-anh/chị, by context |
| formal/official | tôi-ông/bà/quý vị; title + name |

Do not over-assume age/gender from appearance unless needed. In modern settings, *tôi-anh/chị* is often safer than over-specific kinship with strangers.

### 4.3 Workplace And Power

- Subordinate may use *em* or *tôi* depending on company culture, age, and formality.
- Boss may call subordinate *em*, *bạn*, name, *anh/chị*, *cậu/cô*; each has tone.
- Meeting vs private life can use different pairs.

Do not default all directors to *tôi-cô* and all employees to *em-sếp*. Establish workplace culture.

### 4.4 Historical / Chinese-Setting Romance

Use *ta-ngươi, thiếp-chàng, bổn vương, nô tỳ, thiếu gia, phu nhân...* only when the story world truly requires it. Do not randomly mix modern and historical systems.

Check:

- is rank established;
- does one character change title in public/private;
- does suffix fit gender/age/role;
- does transliterated style sound natural for target Vietnamese listeners.

## 5. Address Shift As Dramatic Beat

Address changes when **relationship or communication tactic changes**, not every few mentions.

Meaningful shifts:

1. **Intimate -> distant:** *anh-em* to *tôi-anh* after betrayal.
2. **Distant -> intimate:** *cô Lan* to *Lan* after danger/shared trust.
3. **Respect -> confrontation:** *mẹ-con* to *bà-tôi* only at very strong rupture.
4. **Private -> public:** *em* at home to *cô Trần* before a committee.

Example:

> *“Anh giải thích đi.”*
>
> Vĩnh đưa tờ giấy về phía tôi. *“Em ký trước đã.”*
>
> Tôi đặt bút xuống. *“Tôi hỏi lại: ai đã đổi mẫu xét nghiệm, anh Vĩnh?”*

`em -> tôi` and `anh -> anh Vĩnh` show the wife withdrawing from intimacy and turning the conversation into interrogation.

Use strong forms like `tôi-cô`, `tao-mày`, or bare-name parent address carefully; they may be too harsh for class/context unless built up.

## 6. Naming For Audio

Names must be **audibly distinct**, not only visually different. The project default is Chinese-style Sino-Vietnamese naming (section 7); these six audio criteria apply to all systems:

1. **Different initial sounds:** avoid Lâm/Lăng/Lục in the same thread.
2. **Different syllable rhythm:** Nhiễm / Vân Khê / Cố Thừa Ngôn separates better than Lâm Duy / Tần Quy / Chu Huy.
3. **Avoid near rhymes:** Vân-Vãn, Niên-Nhiên can blur through TTS/small speakers.
4. **Different audible role anchors:** bác sĩ Thẩm / Cố tổng / dì Ôn.
5. **Limit named load:** name only characters who return or need distinction.
6. **TTS stability:** test the intended voice, especially multi-syllable or foreign names.

No universal "max 4-5 names" rule exists. It depends on length, repetition, sound contrast, and role anchoring.

Before naming, fill:

| Character | Needs name? | First anchor | Default reference | Can sound like whom? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| narrator | optional | cô dâu / chị cả | tôi | — |
| twin sister | yes | em gái tôi, Vãn Tinh | Vãn Tinh / em gái tôi | avoid Vân Tinh |
| husband | yes if many men | chồng tôi, Cố Thừa Ngôn | Thừa Ngôn / anh | avoid Thành Ngôn |
| one-scene technician | no | kỹ thuật viên xét nghiệm | anh kỹ thuật viên | — |

Do not name by personality cliche. A "cold" name does not make someone cold; character comes from choices, dialogue, and action.

## 7. Chinese-Style Sino-Vietnamese Names — Project Default

This project **defaults to Chinese-style names** written in Sino-Vietnamese reading familiar to Vietnamese listeners of translated/romance fiction. This is an aesthetic convention, not a universal Vietnamese rule. Narration, dialogue, and address forms still stay Vietnamese according to sections 1-5.

| Context | Naming system |
|---|---|
| Default, unless user says otherwise | Chinese surname + Sino-Vietnamese given name, **modern Chinese setting** (convert style: Chinese-leaning institutions, daily life, generic or Chinese-flavored places) |
| Historical/Chinese fictional setting | Sino-Vietnamese names + correct period titles |
| User explicitly requests Vietnam setting | natural Vietnamese names by region/generation |
| User explicitly requests another country | culture-fitting names; test TTS |

The setting default is as binding as the naming default: both stay Chinese-style until the user explicitly asks otherwise. Do not drift into Vietnamese-coded places, schools, agencies, or holidays while keeping Chinese names.

Direct user requests for setting/naming override this default.

### Standard Chinese-Style Formula

- **Surname:** one syllable from common Chinese surnames: *Lâm, Tô, Cố, Thẩm, Giang, Hàn, Bạch, Tống, Hứa, Tạ, Ôn, Nhiếp, Yến, Tần, Kiều, Lục, Phó, Ninh, Chu, Đường, Mạc...* Surnames like *Trần, Lý, Vương, Trương, Lưu* are valid but overlap common Vietnamese surnames; prefer less-Vietnamese-sounding surnames when the Chinese flavor should be audible immediately.
- **Given name:** one or two syllables; prefer two-syllable names (*Vân Khê, Thừa Ngôn, Dạ Bạch, Cẩm Niên, Vãn Tinh, Thanh Từ, Niệm An...*) for audio clarity and genre flavor.
- **Compound surnames** such as *Mộ Dung, Âu Dương, Thượng Quan* strongly signal historical/wuxia; use only intentionally.
- Still check all names against audio criteria: different initials/rhythm, no near rhyme, stable TTS.

### Signs A Name Is Too Vietnamese For A Chinese-Style Story

- Vietnamese surnames: *Nguyễn, Phạm, Võ, Đặng, Đỗ, Huỳnh...*
- Vietnamese middle-name patterns: *Văn*, *Thị*.
- Modern Vietnamese one-syllable given names: *Tuấn, Hùng, Dũng, Thảo, Trang, Hoa, Lan...* Chinese-style one-syllable names use a different sound layer: *Nhiễm, Uyên, Miên, Niệm, Khê, Hạo...*
- Main cast all called by short Vietnamese-style one-syllable names (*Vĩnh, Hạ, Duy*) instead of two-syllable/whole Chinese-style names.

### Calling Chinese-Style Names

- Intimate: the full two-syllable given name — *“Vân Khê, về ăn cơm.”* — or `tiểu/A + tên`.
- **One-syllable given names take `tiểu/A + tên` when intimate** (*tiểu Nhã, A Vãn*), never the bare syllable: *“Nhã ơi”, “Hân à”* is Vietnamese register and breaks a Chinese-style world. Friends and groupmates who are on warm terms default to `tiểu + tên`.
- Not yet close / newly met / distant: **full surname + given name** — *“Trần Nhã đúng không?”* Moving from full name to `tiểu + tên` or the given name is a relationship beat; spend it deliberately.
- Serious/blaming/formal: full name — *“Lâm Vân Khê, em nói lại lần nữa xem.”*
- Social/work: surname + title — *Cố tổng, bác sĩ Thẩm, luật sư Tống.*
- Affection nickname: *A/Tiểu + one syllable* (*A Vãn, Tiểu Thất*) or a reduplicated pet name (*Viên Viên, Đường Đường*), consistently for one person. Prefer these over inventing minor characters whose entire name is one bare syllable, which reads Vietnamese.
- *X tổng, X thiếu* fit modern wealthy Chinese-style fiction; *lão gia, ma ma* fit historical style. Do not mix layers unless intentional. When the user requests Vietnam setting, do not use these titles just because they sound fancy.
- **Keep Vietnamese pronoun/address system** (*anh-em, tôi-cô, con-mẹ...*) in modern stories. Do not import *ta-ngươi, thiếp-chàng* just because the names are Chinese-style.

Preserve old principles:

- Do not copy sample names from this skill into stories.
- Avoid names chosen only for pretty dictionary meanings if they are hard to hear.
- Examples such as *Lan, Vĩnh, Hạ, Duy, Minh* elsewhere illustrate address/pronoun mechanics; when naming real stories, use this section's default naming unless the user requested otherwise.

### Cultural Synchronization

Names, food, marriage law, address, titles, and institutions must belong to one world. A character named *Tô Vân Khanh* should work at a generic *tập đoàn, bệnh viện thành phố* and call her mother *mẹ*. Having her work at *ủy ban phường* and call mother *má* exposes a stitched world unless deliberate localization is established.

If the user requests a Vietnam setting, change both names and life details to Vietnam; do not keep Chinese names in a Vietnamese world.

## 8. Prevent Ambiguous Pronouns

Audio listeners cannot glance back at previous lines. Every pronoun needs one unique, nearby, logical antecedent.

For each `nó`, `hắn`, `anh ấy`, `cô ấy`, `người đó`, `điều ấy`, ask:

> Do the previous two sentences contain more than one noun that could fit?

If yes, repeat name/role/object.

Demonstratives `này/đó/ấy` also need a clear introduced object/event. Do not say *“cuốn tiểu thuyết ấy”* for a book mentioned for the first time; introduce *“một cuốn tiểu thuyết tôi đọc tối qua”* first.

`Nó` is especially dangerous. It can mean a younger/equal person with intimate or contempt shade, an object, an event, a baby/fetus, or a whole clause.

**Ambiguous:**

> *Đứa bé vừa đạp, còn chồng tôi tin tờ giấy hơn tin chính nó.*

**Clear:**

> *Đứa bé vừa đạp, còn chồng tôi tin kết quả trên tờ xét nghiệm hơn lời tôi.*

**Ambiguous:**

> *Tôi đã đọc cảnh này trong cuốn sách về người vợ sắp chết vì nó.*

**Clear:**

> *Trong cuốn sách, tờ xét nghiệm giả ấy khiến người vợ bị đuổi khỏi nhà và không qua khỏi sau khi sinh.*

Repeating a noun is not a repetition flaw when it saves meaning.

`Hắn/gã/ả` carries attitude; it is not neutral. Use only when contempt, fear, hatred, or objectification fits the POV.

Vietnamese can omit subjects only when the subject continues and cannot be confused:

> *Vĩnh rót trà. Đẩy chén về phía tôi. Không ngẩng lên.*

If another character has entered, restore the subject.

## 9. Multi-Person Scenes

In scenes with three or more people:

- repeat name/role when the acting subject changes;
- use dialogue tags when voice is not distinct;
- give characters position/action anchors;
- avoid chains of `anh ta/cô ấy/người kia`.

Weak:

> *Minh nhìn Duy. Anh ta đưa anh ta phong bì. Hạ bảo anh đừng mở nhưng anh vẫn mở.*

Clear:

> *Minh đưa phong bì cho Duy. Hạ chặn tay anh trai lại, nhưng Duy đã xé mép giấy.*

Use "role + name" at first introduction:

> *Luật sư Duy, người đại diện cho mẹ tôi, đặt cặp hồ sơ xuống.*

Later, use *Duy* or *luật sư* by focus. Do not repeat the full introduction every time.

Vietnamese characters do not use each other's names in every dialogue line.

**Stiff:**

> *“Hạ, em đã làm gì vậy, Hạ?” Vĩnh hỏi. “Vĩnh, anh không hiểu đâu, Vĩnh.”*

**Natural:**

> *“Hạ, em đã làm gì vậy?”*
>
> *“Anh không hiểu đâu.”*

Names pull attention or intensify emotion; they are not punctuation.

## 10. Repair Examples

### Relationship Spoken Like A Profile

**Stiff:**

> *Trần Ngọc Hạ, em gái song sinh của tôi, đứng lên nói rằng cô ấy phản đối cuộc hôn nhân.*

**Natural:**

> *Em gái song sinh của tôi đứng dậy. Hạ không nhìn tôi khi nói: “Em phản đối cuộc hôn nhân này.”*

First orient the relation, then use the name in action.

### Mechanical Rotation

**Stiff:**

> *Vĩnh bước vào. Anh ta đặt giấy xuống. Người đàn ông ấy nhìn tôi. Chồng tôi thở dài.*

**Natural:**

> *Vĩnh bước vào, đặt tờ giấy xuống trước mặt tôi. Anh không ngồi. Chỉ đến khi tôi đọc xong, chồng tôi mới thở dài.*

`chồng tôi` at the end intentionally recalls the threatened relationship.

### Unbridged Address Shift

**Stiff:**

> *“Em yêu anh.” “Tôi không tin cô.”*

**With bridge:**

> *“Em yêu anh.”*
>
> Anh nhìn chữ ký cuối trang, rồi tháo nhẫn đặt cạnh bút. *“Từ giờ, cô nói chuyện với luật sư của tôi.”*

Removing the ring justifies the shift.

### Pretty Names Hard To Hear

**Hard:** Lâm Linh, Lâm Minh, Lăng Ninh in one scene.

**Clearer:** Lâm Vãn, Tạ Thừa Chu, Vệ Thanh Từ — different initial/rhythm, Chinese-style default. These are structural examples; do not copy them into stories.

## 11. Final Review Workflow

### Pass 1 — Pair Map

- [ ] Each main pair has two-way address forms.
- [ ] Public/private or before/after shifts are recorded.
- [ ] Strong shifts have cause and consequence.

### Pass 2 — Names By Ear

- [ ] Names differ by sound and rhythm; TTS reads clearly.
- [ ] One-scene characters are not named unnecessarily.
- [ ] Naming system matches culture; default is Chinese-style unless user requests another.
- [ ] No sample names from this skill are copied.

### Pass 3 — Pronouns

- [ ] Each pronoun has a unique antecedent; `này/đó/ấy` does not point to an unintroduced object.
- [ ] Multi-person scenes repeat name/role on subject switches.
- [ ] `hắn/gã/ả` fits POV tone, not automatic villain label.
- [ ] Omitted subjects do not confuse who acts.

### Pass 4 — Dialogue Read-Aloud

- [ ] Without tags, most speakers are distinguishable.
- [ ] Characters do not say the other person's name every line.
- [ ] Address forms fit age, rank, power, intimacy, and current emotion.
- [ ] No line exists only to explain a relationship to the audience.
